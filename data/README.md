## bin_list.csv

Used by `dte/good_card.py`, `attacks/format_filter.py` and `attacks/side_info.py`.

| column       | meaning                                                                  |
|--------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| `bin`        | Issuer prefix, exactly 6 digits                                          |
| `network`    | `visa` or `mastercard` (16-digit cards only, so no Amex)                 |
| `bank`       | Issuing bank, e.g. `DBS`                                                 |
| `product`    | `credit` or `debit` (the source does not give the card tier)             |
| `weight`     | Positive integer, relative frequency used by the DTE's inverse-CDF       |
| `confidence` | `aggregator` or `illustrative`, see below                                |

**BIN source.** Rows marked `aggregator` are Singapore-issued BINs listed on
creditcardvalidator.org (https://www.creditcardvalidator.org/country/sg-singapore, retrieved
2026-10-17). 462845 is attributed to DBS by greip.io (https://greip.io/tools/BIN-Lookup/462845),
because creditcardvalidator.org does not name its bank. These are third-party lookups, not issuer
documentation. They are real prefixes as far as public BIN databases go, but treat the bank
attribution as best-effort. POSB is DBS's consumer brand and is kept as listed.

Rows marked `illustrative` are made up: two Mastercard 2-series prefixes (`2221xx`-`2720xx`), so
that range is represented. (Two made-up UOB Visa BINs were removed on 2026-10-17, because the table
already has real UOB Mastercard BINs.) They have valid network prefixes but are not known to be real BINs. Don't
present them as real.

**Weights: our modelling assumption, not measured data.** No public source gives per-BIN card
counts. We chose integer weights so that:
- Visa totals 600 and Mastercard 400 (out of 1000), i.e. an assumed 60/40 network split;
- within a network, larger banks (DBS/POSB, OCBC, UOB) get more weight than smaller issuers.

The table has 20 BINs: 18 `aggregator`, 2 `illustrative`.

The chi-square test in `tests/test_good_card_dte.py` checks only that the DTE matches these weights.
It says nothing about whether the weights match reality. That gap is exactly the "distribution
mismatch" an attacker with a better model of real cards could exploit.

The weights must stay integers: the DTE gives BIN *i* exactly `weight_i` of `sum(weights)` slots, which
is what keeps encoding exactly reversible (see the `dte/good_card.py` docstring).

## pin_frequencies.csv

Used by `attacks/pin_ranking.py` (the attacker's guess order) and to sample victim PINs in
experiments (`sample_pins`). Generated by `python data/build_pin_frequencies.py`. A test checks that
the committed CSV matches the script's output.

| column      | meaning                                                              |
|-------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| `pin`       | 4-digit string, leading zeros kept (`0000`)                          |
| `frequency` | Probability of a user choosing this PIN; the 10,000 values sum to 1  |
| `tier`      | `published`, `pattern` or `base`: where the number comes from        |

**Source.** Nick Berry, "PIN number analysis", DataGenetics, September 2012,
https://datagenetics.com/blog/september32012/index.html (retrieved 2026-10-17). About 3.4 million
4-digit passwords from leaked password lists, used as a proxy for PINs. Cite this in the slides.

**Published numbers (used exactly as given):**
- the top 20 PINs with their frequencies, from 1234 = 10.713% down to 1010 = 0.285% (26.83% in total);
- the 21 least popular PINs (ranks #9980-#10000), from 8557 = 0.001191% down to 8068 = 0.000744%;
- the 426 most popular PINs together cover 50% (used to calibrate the model, see below).

The article also gives checks we did *not* fit to, so we can see how far off the model is: the top 61
PINs cover about one third, and 1234 alone is more common than the bottom 4,200 PINs combined.

**Our assumption: how the other 9,959 PINs are spread.** The article names patterns people use
(years such as 19xx, dates in MMDD form, repeated pairs such as 0101 or 4545) but gives no numbers
for them. We model two tiers:

| tier      | PINs                                                                 | probability each |
|-----------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------|
| `pattern` | valid MMDD dates (0101-1231, incl. 0229), years 1900-2029, ABAB pairs, minus published PINs (564 PINs) | `k·b` ≈ 0.0571% |
| `base`    | every other PIN (9,395 PINs)                                         | `b` ≈ 0.00436%   |

`b` and `k` (≈ 13) are not guessed: they are solved from two published facts, that the total is 1 and
that the top 426 cover 50% (ranks 21-426 are all pattern PINs). The only assumption is *which* PINs
count as patterns.

**How far off the model is, and in which direction:**

| statistic                | published  | calibrated model | uniform-tail model |
|--------------------------|------------|------------------|--------------------|
| top 61 PINs cover        | about 33%  | 29.2%            | 27.1%              |
| top 426 PINs cover       | 50%        | 50.0% (fitted)   | 29.8%              |
| bottom 4,200 PINs cover  | < 10.7%    | 18.2%            | 30.7%              |

Both models put too much probability on unpopular PINs, i.e. they understate how concentrated real
PINs are. An attacker using the real distribution would do at least as well as ours, so attack
success measured with this table is a **conservative lower bound**.

**Sensitivity check:** `pin_frequencies_uniform_tail.csv` keeps the same 41 published values and
spreads the rest uniformly. Load it with `load_pin_frequencies(UNIFORM_TAIL_PIN_PATH)`.

**Limitations to mention in Q&A:** these are website passwords from around 2012, mostly from
English-language sites, not Singapore bank PINs. Some banks block very common PINs such as 1234,
which would lower the top frequencies.
